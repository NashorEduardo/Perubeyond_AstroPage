Planning a trip to the land of the Incas without checking the calendar is one of the most common mistakes. **Public holidays in Peru** (national holidays, non-working days and regional festivals) can completely change your experience: they can fill the trains to Machu Picchu with Peruvian families, multiply hotel prices in Cusco, close museums and banks for a day, or give you the chance to witness a celebration you will not see at any other time of the year.

The short answer: **Peru has 16 national holidays a year**, and the ones that affect travellers most are **Holy Week, Independence Days (July 28 and 29), Christmas and New Year**. On top of them come the big Cusco festivals: Corpus Christi, Inti Raymi on June 24, the Virgen del Carmen in Paucartambo and Qoyllur Rit'i. In this **Peru Beyond** guide we show you the 2026-2027 calendar, the real impact of each date on your trip and how to book so you avoid surprises (or turn them to your advantage).

> **📌 Key Fact:** The data in this article is current as of November 2026. Holiday and non-working-day dates are set by the State and can change by decree: **always verify on gob.pe and in the official gazette El Peruano before buying flights or booking**.

Before going into detail, it helps to clear up three concepts that people often mix up:

- **National holiday:** a date set by law, with mandatory paid rest across the country.
- **Non-working day:** an exceptional measure by the Executive branch, normally for the public sector, with hours later made up. In the private sector it is optional and depends on agreement between employer and workers.
- **Regional holiday or festival:** a celebration specific to a region or city (for example, those of Cusco). It creates local activity and closures even if it is not a national holiday.

For tourists the distinction matters because trains, hotels and tickets fill up because **Peruvians want to travel on long weekends**, whether it is a holiday, a non-working day or a bridge.

### 2.2. Non-working days and bridges: the surprise factor

Besides the fixed holidays, the government can decree **compensable non-working days**, which often create "bridges" and long weekends. A recent example: **Monday, July 27, 2026** was declared a non-working day for the public sector under Supreme Decree No. 075-2026-PCM. Together with Saturday 25, Sunday 26 and the holidays of the 28th and 29th, it formed a **five-day long weekend** designed to encourage domestic tourism. For the private sector the measure was not automatic: it depended on agreement between employers and workers. Essential services, transport, airports and restaurants kept running as normal.

The lesson for travellers: **a non-working day can appear with only a few weeks' notice**, and its effect on tourist demand is that of a holiday. That is why it pays to book with some margin and to choose flexible-change rates whenever possible.

### 2.3. Will holidays move to Fridays in 2027?

In August 2026 the Ministry of Economy and Finance announced its intention to move most holidays to Fridays during 2027, aiming to boost domestic tourism and reduce lost productivity. As reported at the time, these would keep their dates: January 1, Holy Thursday and Good Friday, July 23, Independence Days (July 28 and 29), August 6, October 8, December 9 and Christmas. The holidays that would move are Labor Day, June 7, Saint Peter and Saint Paul, Saint Rose of Lima, All Saints' Day and the Immaculate Conception.

**Important:** as far as we could verify, this is an announcement and not a published rule. If approved, there would be more Friday-to-Sunday long weekends in the 2027 calendar. If your 2027 trip touches those dates, confirm the rule in force in El Peruano before booking.

> **💡 Peru Beyond Tip:** When you compare quotes for 2027, ask that the proposal flag which dates in your itinerary coincide with national or regional holidays. A good adviser should point this out, because it affects price, opening hours and availability.

## 4. Independence Days (July 28 and 29): The Peak of the Year

**Fiestas Patrias** commemorate Peru's independence and are the most important holiday on the calendar. They fall in the heart of the Andean winter: dry season, clear skies in Cusco, which attracts international visitors and, at the same time, hundreds of thousands of Peruvians who use the long weekend to travel. That combination explains the phenomenon.

**What usually happens on these dates:**

- **Machu Picchu tickets:** daily quotas are limited by the Ministry of Culture and, in July and August, they sell out far in advance. In 2026 the press reported that even in-person sales in Machu Picchu Pueblo sold out on high-demand days, and that since May 2026 they can be bought up to three days ahead (check the current system on the official platform).
- **Trains:** services to Machu Picchu and the most convenient departure times fill up earlier than during the rest of the year.
- **Hotels in Cusco and the Sacred Valley:** occupancy rises and rates move up. The best-located hotels sell out first.
- **Lima:** outbound and return traffic from domestic travellers saturates highways and terminals. Airports and long-distance buses are in high demand.
- **Closures:** museums and public offices may close on July 28 and 29 or change hours. Restaurants and agencies usually operate.

> **💡 Peru Beyond Tip:** If your travel dates include Independence Days, book the Machu Picchu tickets and the train first, and only then the rest of the itinerary. Everything else (hotels, Sacred Valley tours) fits around those two quotas.

## 5. Holy Week: Processions, Faith and a Long Weekend

**Holy Week** has a movable date: in 2026 it ran from March 29 to April 5 (Holy Thursday on April 2 and Good Friday on April 3), and in 2027 it falls from March 21 to 28 (Holy Thursday on the 25th and Good Friday on the 26th). Thursday and Friday are national holidays, and most Peruvians take the chance to travel.

**In Cusco** it has a unique protagonist: the **Lord of the Earthquakes (Señor de los Temblores)**, whose procession leaves on Holy Monday (March 30 in 2026) through the historic centre and ends in the Plaza de Armas. The Holy Thursday visit to the Seven Churches and the Good Friday Way of the Cross also stand out.

**What to expect as a traveller:**

- Heavy crowds in churches and the streets of the historic centre, with some pedestrian routes cut off by processions.
- Some shops and services with reduced hours on Holy Thursday and Good Friday.
- High occupancy in hotels and trains, although the highlands are at the end of the rainy season.

It is an excellent date if you are interested in religious and cultural heritage, and a poor choice if you are looking for quiet corners.

### 6.1. Inti Raymi (June 24)

The **Festival of the Sun** recreates the Inca winter solstice ceremony. There is a ceremony at Qorikancha in the morning, a procession through the Plaza de Armas and the main scene on the Sacsayhuaman esplanade, which requires a paid ticket. It is the date with the greatest concentration of visitors in Cusco: full hotels, tours with limited seats and higher rates. Book very early and arrive early at the viewing points.

### 6.2. Corpus Christi

It is celebrated 60 days after Easter. On the eve, the images from the parishes arrive at the Plaza de Armas, and the next day the main procession goes to the Cathedral. It also has a culinary side (chiri uchu, the festival's typical dish). It is an ideal festival to see Cusco's popular faith, but you need to arrive early and be patient with street closures.

### 6.3. Qoyllur Rit'i

This is a pilgrimage of thousands of faithful above 4,800 m, next to the Sinakara snowcap. It is a spiritual event, not a tourist attraction. If it interests you, go with a reputable agency, with at least two days of prior acclimatisation in Cusco, very warm clothing and absolute respect for the ceremony.

### 6.4. Virgen del Carmen in Paucartambo

From July 15 to 18, the town of Paucartambo (a few hours from Cusco) fills with dance troupes, music and processions in honor of the Mamacha Carmen. It falls just before Independence Days, so **July concentrates two demand peaks within a few weeks**. Lodging in the town is very limited, so many visitors do it as an excursion from Cusco.

### 6.5. Santurantikuy (December 24)

This is the handicraft and Christmas-figure fair in the Plaza de Armas. Many vendors do not accept cards, so carry soles in cash. It is ideal for buying souvenirs with Cusco identity.

## 7. All Saints, Christmas and New Year

**All Saints' Day (November 1)** is a national holiday, and **November 2** (Day of the Dead) is not a national holiday but is lived through cemetery visits, traditions and meals. For a traveller the impact is medium: some services close, but the tourism offer operates. It is a good chance to learn about Peruvian customs, with the rains beginning in the highlands.

**December** is a high-demand month: the Immaculate Conception (December 8) and the Battle of Ayacucho (December 9) form a block early in the month; then come Christmas (December 25) and New Year. It is the rainy season in the highlands, but with many school and family holidays. Hotels in central Cusco and Lima fill up, and some services (such as certain trains) may adjust schedules. Book well in advance, especially if you want to spend New Year's Eve in Cusco.

## 8. Real Effects of Holidays on Your Trip

### 8.1. Closures: museums, banks and shops

| Service | Usual behavior on a national holiday | What to do |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Public offices** | Closed | Avoid paperwork on those dates |
| **Banks** | Many branches close; ATMs keep working | Withdraw cash ahead of time |
| **Museums and archaeological sites** | Some change hours or close, others open | Check official hours before going |
| **Machu Picchu** | Operates according to the quota and the time slots on your ticket | Buy in advance; tickets are not sold "on the fly" |
| **Restaurants and agencies** | Operate, with high demand | Book a table on key dates |
| **Airports and transport** | Operate with more passengers | Arrive with extra time |

### 8.2. Prices and occupancy

On high-demand festive dates, **hotels, trains and tours go up in price and sell out earlier**. Machu Picchu and train quotas are limited and cannot be expanded at will, so they are the real bottleneck of the trip. We do not give price figures because they vary each year and by category: always request an up-to-date quote.

### 8.3. Machu Picchu and trains

The Ministry of Culture sets the circuits, time slots and daily quotas. High season runs roughly from June to October (festive periods included), and on high-demand dates tickets for the most sought-after circuits sell out months ahead. Trains have fewer seats than travellers, and the most convenient departure times go first. As a practical reference, for high season and major holidays it is wise to buy **4 to 6 months ahead**; confirm the current sales system on the official platform.

### 8.4. Traffic and transport in Lima

On the eve of long weekends, the exit from Lima via highways and terminals gets congested, and so does the return on the last day. If you have a flight or connection on a holiday, **leave for the airport with more time** than usual and avoid scheduling ground transfers at peak departure and return hours.

> **💡 Peru Beyond Tip:** If you land in Lima the day before a long weekend, consider a private transfer arranged from the airport. With heavy traffic, the punctuality of an assigned driver is worth more than saving a few soles.

## 11. Conclusion: Plan with the Calendar in Hand

**Public holidays in Peru** are not an obstacle, they are a planning variable. With 16 national holidays a year, non-working days that can appear on short notice and very crowded regional festivals, the key is to know which date you choose and to secure the scarcest quotas first: Machu Picchu and the train. If you want peace and quiet, travel between April and May or in September and October.
