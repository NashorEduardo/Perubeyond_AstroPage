If you are Mexican and planning a trip to the land of the Incas, you have a real head start: we share a language, a mestizo heritage, a love of food, corn and chili, patron saint festivals and a warm way of welcoming visitors. That is why **Peruvian customs for Mexican travelers** are rarely a culture shock. They are a handful of small nuances that, once you know them, make your trip smoother and more respectful.

The short answer to "what should I know?" is this: **always say hello, ask before photographing people, bargain only where it is appropriate, respect the coca leaf and the Pachamama, mind your slang, bring an adapter (and check the voltage), and follow the Machu Picchu rules to the letter.** In this **Peru Beyond** guide we walk through each point with concrete examples, a Peru vs Mexico table, a glossary of words that change meaning, and a timeline of a typical day in Cusco.

> **📌 Key Fact:** Peru and Mexico are two of the great pre-Hispanic heritages of the Americas. That affinity will make you feel at home, but remember that Peru has a coast, the Andes and the Amazon, and each region has its own codes. This guide focuses on what you will find in Lima, Cusco, the Sacred Valley and Machu Picchu.

## 1. Peru and Mexico: More Alike Than You Think

Before the differences, it is worth recognizing what you already share with Peruvians. In both countries people linger at the table, share meals with family, celebrate patron saints with music and processions, and value hospitality. A Mexican visitor tends to connect right away with someone from Cusco or Lima.

What changes are the details: vocabulary, how time is handled in some contexts, the rules of certain sacred places, and the relationship with Andean tradition. The table below summarizes the main similarities and differences.

### 1.1. Comparison Table: Customs in Peru vs Mexico

| Topic | In Peru | In Mexico | What to do as a traveler |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Greeting** | A cheek kiss between women and between a woman and a man in social settings; handshake in formal ones. In Andean communities, a soft, respectful greeting | Cheek kiss or hug; handshake in formal settings | Greet people when entering shops, restaurants and taxis; follow the other person's lead |
| **Form of address** | "Usted" is common with older people and in services; "caserita/o" in markets | "Usted" and "tú" depending on context; "joven", "güerito/a" in markets | Default to "usted", especially with elders and local authorities |
| **Punctuality** | "Peruvian time" is flexible at social gatherings, but tours, trains and Machu Picchu entry slots run on strict schedules | "Mexican time" is flexible socially | Be on time for anything with a ticket or a guide |
| **Tipping** | Voluntary; around 10% in restaurants (check if service is included); guides and drivers appreciate a tip | More widespread, commonly 10-15% | Carry small change |
| **Bargaining** | Normal in markets and with artisans; not in restaurants, supermarkets or formal shops | Normal in tianguis and markets | Ask for a discount with a smile and without pressure |
| **Main meal** | **Almuerzo** (around noon to 3 p.m.) is the big meal; the "menú" is a fixed-price lunch | **Comida** (2-4 p.m.) is the main meal; "almuerzo" is a late breakfast | Enjoy the midday menu and eat lighter at night |
| **Spice** | **Ají** is on the table but usually served separately, in small dishes | **Chile** goes in almost everything | Try ají a little at a time; ask before adding it |
| **Currency** | Peruvian sol (S/) | Mexican peso | Exchange at exchange offices or ATMs; check the day's rate |
| **Electricity** | 220 V, 60 Hz, outlets A, B and C | 127 V, 60 Hz, outlets A and B | Check your devices' voltage |
| **Altitude** | Cusco at 3,399 m | Mexico City at about 2,240 m | Acclimatize: soroche does not care about nationality |
| **Festivals** | Lively Andean folk religiosity (Corpus Christi, Inti Raymi, Señor de los Milagros) | Equally lively folk religiosity (Day of the Dead, Guadalupe) | Observe respectfully and ask before photographing |

## 2. Greetings, Courtesy and Good Manners

In Peru, **greeting is essential**. Walking into a shop, a small restaurant or a taxi without saying "buenos días" or "buenas tardes" can feel cold. A "gracias, que le vaya bien" on the way out is appreciated. As a Mexican you already do this at home, so just keep it up.

- **Usted as the rule:** with older people, guides, authorities and in Andean communities, use "usted". With people your own age, "tú" is also normal.
- **Handshakes:** in Andean towns they are usually soft and brief. A light grip does not mean disinterest; it is a form of respect.
- **Quechua words:** an "Allillanchu" (hello, how are you?) or "Sulpayki" (thank you) brings real smiles. You do not need perfect pronunciation.
- **Ayni:** the Andean principle of reciprocity. If someone helps you, you give something back. It is the foundation of hospitality in rural communities.

> **💡 Peru Beyond Tip:** If a vendor calls you "caserito" or "caserita", it is a friendly term meaning "my regular customer". Answering with a smile and a "buenas, ¿cuánto cuesta?" opens the conversation better than going straight to the price.

The train to Machu Picchu leaves on time, entry to the citadel happens in an assigned slot, and morning tours depart early.

## 4. Tipping and Bills: How to Say Thanks Without Stress

Tipping in Peru is a gesture of thanks, not an obligation. In Mexico you may be used to leaving 10% to 15%. In Peru you can follow a similar pattern, with these notes:

1. **Restaurants:** check whether the bill already includes "servicio". If not and the service was good, around 10% is a common habit.
2. **Guides and drivers:** a tip at the end of the tour is widely practiced and greatly appreciated. There is no official amount.
3. **Hotels:** leave some change for housekeeping or for whoever helps with your bags.
4. **Taxis:** with informal taxis the fare is agreed beforehand; no extra tip is expected.

> **💡 Peru Beyond Tip:** Carry small bills and sol coins. Tipping and shopping in markets with large bills makes change hard to find, and some businesses reject torn or badly damaged notes.

## 5. Bargaining in Markets: When Yes and When No

If you are Mexican, bargaining is nothing new: in the tianguis and the market, prices are negotiated. In Peru it is similar, with nuances:

- **Do bargain:** craft markets, fairs, textile and souvenir stalls, and sometimes street vendors.
- **Do not bargain:** restaurants, supermarkets, fixed-price shops, entrance tickets, trains and agency services.
- **With respect:** ask for a reasonable discount, do not insist if they say no, and avoid offering an absurdly low price. A hand-woven piece takes days of work.
- **Buy wisely:** ask about the material. There is a difference between an alpaca garment and one made of synthetic fiber, and the prices and quality reflect it.

A good practice is to buy directly from weavers in workshops or cooperatives, where the income reaches the people who make the pieces. Your Peru Beyond guide can recommend trustworthy places.

## 6. Slang: Words That Change Between Peru and Mexico

This is the point that surprises people most. We share a language, but not every meaning. Here is a guide to frequent words (always keeping in mind that colloquial Spanish varies by region and generation).

| Word | In Peru | In Mexico | Watch out for... |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Pata** | Close friend ("mi pata") | An animal's or furniture's leg; "pata de perro" is someone who wanders a lot | Calling someone "pata" in Peru is a sign of trust, not a reference to their leg |
| **Chamba** | Work, a job (colloquial) | Work (colloquial); also "de chamba" meaning by luck | A rare case where both countries agree |
| **Palta** | Avocado | Not used; it is aguacate | If you ask for "aguacate" in Peru, you will be understood, but they will say palta |
| **Choclo** | Large-kernel tender corn, very Andean | Elote | Peruvian choclo is bigger, with soft kernels |
| **Cancha** | Toasted corn eaten as a snack | A playing field (cancha de futbol) | On a Peruvian table, "cancha" is a snack |
| **Mozo** | Waiter | Rarely used; it is mesero | To get a waiter's attention, "disculpe, joven" or "mozo" both work |
| **Ají** | Hot chili pepper, the base of the sauce | Chile | Do not ask for "chile"; say "ají" or "salsa de ají" |
| **Combi** | Public transport minibus | Combi, microbús or pesero (depending on the city) | For tourists, formal or agency transport is better |
| **Chela** | Beer | Beer | The same word in both countries |
| **Jato** | Home, house (colloquial) | Not common | Someone may say "te espero en mi jato" |
| **Al toque** | Right away | Not used | If they say "al toque", it means "right now" |
| **Bacán / chévere** | Great, very good | "Padre", "chido" | "Qué bacán" is a compliment |
| **Soroche** | Altitude sickness | Altitude sickness (less common) | A key word in Cusco |
| **Cachar** | In popular Peruvian use it has a vulgar connotation | To catch or to spot something (cachar la pelota, "te cacharon") | Avoid it; in Peru it can sound offensive |
| **Ya** | "Okay", "agreed" | Means "already" or "now" | "Ya, ya" in Peru can be "yes, understood" |
| **Mande** | Not used | Polite way to ask someone to repeat | In Peru say "¿cómo dijo?" or "¿perdón?" |

> **📌 Key Fact:** The safest expressions are those tied to food and greetings. If you are unsure about a colloquial word, use the neutral version; nobody will be offended by a "perdone, ¿cómo dijo?".

### 6.1. Food: Names That Can Confuse You

- **Menú:** in Peru it is a fixed-price lunch (starter, main and sometimes a drink). It is not the menu card.
- **Chifa:** a restaurant serving Chinese-Peruvian cooking, one of the country's best-loved food traditions.
- **Causa, ceviche, lomo saltado, ají de gallina, cuy:** emblematic dishes. In Cusco, cuy is a ceremonial and festive specialty; if it makes you uncomfortable, you are not obliged to try it.
- **Jugo:** in Peru it is fresh blended fruit juice; plentiful and delicious.

## 7. Pachamama and the Coca Leaf: Respect for the Sacred

Two elements that may not be part of your daily life in Mexico are central in the Andes: the **Pachamama** (Mother Earth) and the **coca leaf**.

### 7.1. The Pachamama

In many communities and festivals, offerings are made to the Pachamama, known as a "pago a la tierra" or "despacho". Before drinking, some people pour a few drops onto the ground as thanks.

- **Observe with respect:** if you witness an offering, do not touch the elements, move them, or photograph them without permission.
- **Ask before recording:** a ceremony is not a show; it is a spiritual act.
- **Join if invited:** if a local guide or a family invites you to make an offering, accepting is a gesture of respect.

### 7.2. The Coca Leaf

The coca leaf is an Andean cultural symbol. It is chewed (chacchado), drunk as tea and used in rituals. **It is not cocaine**, and its traditional use is part of life in the Andes.

- **Coca tea:** an infusion offered in hotels and restaurants, associated with coping with altitude.
- **Not for taking home:** we do not recommend taking leaves or coca products out of the country; customs laws elsewhere, including Mexico, can differ. Check with your own country's customs authority before buying.
- **Medical advice:** if you have a health condition or take medication, ask your doctor before drinking coca tea.

## 8. Photographing People and Traditional Dress

In Cusco and the Sacred Valley it is common to see women and children in traditional dress and sometimes with llamas or lambs near plazas and tourist sites. Many of them offer to pose for photos as part of their work.

- **Always ask permission:** with a gesture or a "¿puedo tomarle una foto?".
- **A tip of a few soles** is a common custom when you pose with them and their animals. It is a way of recognizing their work.
- **If they say no, respect it:** never insist or photograph from a distance without them noticing.
- **Children and ceremonies:** ask the adults first and avoid photos during rituals or processions if you are unsure.
- **They are not scenery:** they are people with their own culture and dignity. A short conversation completely changes the photo.

## 9. Cusco and Altitude: What a Mexican Traveler Should Know

If you come from a high city such as Mexico City (about 2,240 m), your body is somewhat used to it, but **Cusco sits at 3,399 m** and most of the Sacred Valley is between 2,800 and 3,000 m. Machu Picchu, at 2,430 m, is lower than Cusco.

**Basic recommendations:**

2. Hydrate well and eat lightly.

> **💡 Peru Beyond Tip:** A good strategy is to sleep first in the Sacred Valley (lower than Cusco) and then go up to Cusco. Your body acclimatizes gradually. Ask about this option when you request your itinerary.

## 10. Festivals and Folk Religiosity

In Mexico, folk religiosity is lived with music, flowers, food and processions. In Peru it is very similar: patron saint festivals, processions and Andean celebrations combine Catholic and pre-Hispanic traditions.

- **Corpus Christi (Cusco):** a procession of saints' images leaving their churches for the main plaza.
- **Inti Raymi (June 24):** a staging of the Festival of the Sun in Cusco.
- **Señor de los Milagros (October, Lima):** a massive procession with purple robes.
- **Fiestas Patrias (July 28 and 29):** celebrating Peru's independence.

If you coincide with a celebration, **observe respectfully**, do not block processions, and ask before photographing participants. Dates and times change every year; check the calendar before you travel.

## 11. Machu Picchu Etiquette: Official Rules

Machu Picchu is a historic sanctuary and a sacred place. The Ministry of Culture publishes a code of conduct with clear prohibitions. These are the most important ones (check the current list on the official site before traveling):

| What | Rule |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Backpacks and bags** | No larger than 40 x 35 x 20 cm |
| **Food and utensils** | Food, plates, cutlery and thermoses are not allowed |
| **Tripods and selfie sticks** | Prohibited (tripods, monopods, selfie sticks) |
| **Umbrellas and walking sticks** | Prohibited (also portable chairs) |
| **Drones** | Prohibited |
| **Noise** | No shouting, whistling, clapping or singing; no instruments or speakers |
| **Footwear** | No high heels or hard soles |
| **Stones and walls** | Do not touch or lean on them; do not run or jump |
| **Circuits** | Do not leave the marked routes |
| **Other** | No smoking or vaping; no alcohol; no baby strollers |

## 12. Currency, Exchange and Payments

Peru's currency is the **sol (S/)**, with the code PEN. There are coins and notes in several denominations. We do not quote a fixed exchange rate because it changes daily: check the current rate with your bank or an exchange office before traveling.

- **Cash:** needed in markets, local transport, tips and small businesses.
- **Card:** widely accepted in hotels, restaurants and shops in tourist areas.
- **ATMs:** common in cities; tell your bank about your trip and check the fees.
- **Notes:** many shops refuse torn or badly damaged notes; take care of the ones you receive.
- **Dollars:** often accepted in tourist areas, but paying in soles is better.

## 13. Plugs and Voltage: The Most Important Warning

This is the point that causes the most damage for Mexican travelers. **Peru uses 220 V at 60 Hz; Mexico uses 127 V at 60 Hz.** The frequency matches, but the voltage is almost double.

| | Peru | Mexico |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Voltage** | 220 V | 127 V |
| **Frequency** | 60 Hz | 60 Hz |
| **Plugs** | Types A, B and C (many outlets accept several types) | Types A and B |
| **Risk** | A 127 V-only device can be damaged | A 220 V-only device will not work well |

**How to protect yourself:**

1. **Read the label:** if it says INPUT 100-240 V (phones, laptops, cameras, modern chargers), you can use it with a simple adapter.
2. **Careful with heat appliances:** hair dryers, straighteners, curlers and some shavers are often single-voltage. Bring them only if they are dual voltage.
3. **Adapter:** bring a universal one or an A/C type. Tourist hotels usually have combined outlets.
4. **Power strip:** in rooms with few outlets, an extension solves the problem.

> **💡 Peru Beyond Tip:** If you are not sure about a device, do not plug it in. Ask at reception: many hotels lend hair dryers.

## 14. Basic Safety: Common Sense and Peace of Mind

The southern tourist corridor (Lima, Cusco, Sacred Valley and Machu Picchu) welcomes visitors all year. As in any destination, common sense helps:

- Use taxis requested by app or through your hotel, and agree on the fare before getting in if the taxi is informal.
- In crowded markets, wear your backpack in front and avoid taking out valuables.
- Do not leave belongings unattended on buses or in restaurants.
- The **Tourist Police (POLTUR)** and the **iPerú** service provide guidance to visitors.
- Book tours with formal agencies and authorized guides.

For a full guide on this topic, see our article on safety when traveling to Peru.

## 16. Final Checklist for the Mexican Traveler

1. Always greet people and use "usted" with elders.
2. Carry small bills and soles.
3. Check your devices' voltage and pack an adapter.
4. Ask permission to photograph and carry change for tips.
5. Rest on your first day at altitude and drink coca tea if you like.
6. Do not bring coca leaves home without checking the rules.
7. Mind your slang (pata, cancha, cachar) to avoid misunderstandings.
8. Respect Machu Picchu's rules and the marked circuits.
9. Verify festival dates and entry requirements with official sources.
